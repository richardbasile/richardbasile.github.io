Thresholds of Endurance

There's a trap almost every runner falls into: training hard enough to feel tired, but never quite hard enough, or easy enough, to actually get better. This is the gray zone, the band between genuinely easy and genuinely hard where effort piles up without much to show for it. Staying out of that trap comes down to three things: the two physiological thresholds that mark your real zones, two different ways to structure a week around them, and how to figure out which one fits you.

Threshold Definitions

Two physiological boundaries anchor everything that follows:

  • Aerobic threshold (LT1): where easy effort tips into something harder.
  • Lactate threshold (LT2): where hard effort stops being sustainable, the point where lactate builds up in your blood faster than your body can clear it.

LT1 and LT2 aren't two separate systems, one chemical and one respiratory. They're the same transition seen from two angles, and "anaerobic threshold" is usually just another name for LT2. Treat your own numbers as estimates of a zone of transition rather than a hairline: different tests can each return a slightly different result.

A simple tool for locating yourself without any of that is Rate of Perceived Exertion, a 1-to-10 scale for how hard something feels. One is barely moving; ten is a sprint you couldn't hold for another second. RPE tracks reasonably well with where you sit relative to your thresholds, which makes it a useful monitor-free gut check.

These two thresholds split effort into three zones:

Easy effort is everything below LT1: your all-day pace, RPE 2–3. You finish feeling like you could've gone harder and further.

The gray zone is the band between LT1 and LT2: harder than comfortable, not hard enough to actually build much, RPE 4–6. Most recreational runners accidentally spend their easy days here, because heart-rate-percentage formulas often place "easy" above LT1 without it feeling like it.

Hard effort is everything above LT2: labored breathing, no conversation, RPE 7–9. This is where the ceiling gets raised.

The simplest way to estimate LT2 without a lab is a 30-minute time trial: run as hard and steady as you can hold, and use your average heart rate over the final 20 minutes. This is a good proxy, since a study comparing this field test against lab lactate testing found the two produced statistically similar results.2 Your aerobic threshold (LT1) sits roughly 80–90% of that number, with 85% a reasonable starting point, though feel is the better final judge: if the effort is genuinely easy and conversational, you're below LT1, whatever the watch says.

Training Models: Polarized vs. Pyramidal

Knowing where your thresholds sit tells you the zone boundaries. It's a separate question how much time to spend in each one, with two well-supported answers.

Polarized Training

Polarized training spends most of its time at the two extremes and skips the middle almost entirely: a large volume of easy running below LT1, a small, concentrated dose of very hard work above LT2, almost nothing in the gray zone between them. Research tracking elite endurance athletes across cross-country skiers, rowers, and runners found this pattern showing up consistently: roughly 80% easy and 20% hard, gray zone essentially empty.1 Plotted as time against intensity, the shape looks like a dumbbell: heavy on both ends, nearly empty in the middle.

Pyramidal Training

Pyramidal training also starts with a large easy base, but instead of jumping straight to a small hard cap, it stacks a deliberate slice of moderate-intensity (tempo-range) work in between, with the smallest volume reserved for the hardest efforts on top. Plotted the same way, it looks like its name suggests: wide base, narrower middle, small peak. A review of training-distribution data across elite endurance athletes, plus a separate review of elite rowers, found pyramidal patterns turning up more often than strict polarized ones.7

The gray-zone warning above still applies here, just with an important distinction. The gray zone is accidental moderate effort, easy runs that quietly drift into "comfortably hard" territory without being planned that way. Pyramidal training's moderate-intensity work is deliberate: it's the tempo runs covered later on, programmed with a purpose, not an easy run that got away from you. Both models agree on the same underlying rule: know which zone you're in, and don't let unplanned effort creep into the moderate range.

Which Model Fits You

Neither model is universally correct. Both outperform a threshold-heavy approach that spends most of its time in the gray zone, fairly consistently.910 Which fits you better depends on experience, race distance, and time of season.

Experience. Building a consistent aerobic base matters most early on, before chasing marginal VO2max gains from hard intervals makes sense, a reasonable case for newer runners leaning pyramidal. This is coaching logic more than a direct finding, since the controlled research comparing the two models is conducted on well-trained runners.

Race distance and time of season. How you should shift your training as race day approaches depends heavily on your race distance, because "race pace" means something different at each one. For 5K and 10K runners, race pace sits well above LT2, so sharpening means adding more hard, high-intensity work. A 16-week study of well-trained runners found switching from pyramidal to polarized partway through produced the best 5K results of any approach tested.8 For half marathon and marathon runners, race pace sits close to LT2 instead, in tempo territory, so sharpening means the opposite: more race-pace, tempo-range volume, pulling the week toward pyramidal as the race nears. A review integrating published research with elite coaching practice across both track and marathon specialists confirms the split: race-pace volume rises for both groups near competition, but since race pace itself sits in a different zone for each, that rise pulls a 5K runner toward polarized and a marathoner toward pyramidal.11

Application

The Easy Run: Building the Engine

A true easy run is ego-crushing. It doesn't feel like training. That feeling is the signal you're in the right zone.

Below LT1, the adaptations are all aerobic: mitochondria multiply, muscles get better at burning fat, capillary density increases. None of it is driven by effort or happens quickly. It comes from volume and consistency at the right intensity. Going harder doesn't speed this up; it just taxes the engine without building it.

The trap is mostly psychological: running slower than feels acceptable is uncomfortable, so the temptation is to nudge into something that quietly counts as work. That's the gray zone: comfortable enough to feel productive, unproductive enough to just pile up fatigue. If you're putting in consistent miles without seeing gains, this is usually the leak.

Heart-rate percentage formulas are a rough guide at best for finding LT1. A better everyday check is the talk test: a full, comfortable conversation means you're below threshold, short phrases mean you've likely crossed it. It's a solid practical proxy: research validating the talk test against lab-measured thresholds found it tracks closely, though the point where talking gets uncomfortable lands a touch above LT1 rather than exactly on it.3 Solid enough for daily use, but a lab test is still the more precise measurement.

As aerobic fitness builds, your easy pace at the same heart rate and effort gradually increases, a slow drift in what "easy" feels like, tracked over months rather than days, that signals your base is building.

Cardiac drift, heat, poor sleep, and accumulated fatigue all push heart rate up at a given pace without anything going wrong. On runs past 90 minutes, start slower than feels necessary to bank headroom, and let pace fall off as drift sets in rather than fighting it. On bad days, slow down further or take walk breaks; if heart rate won't come down no matter how easy you go, that's a signal for a rest day instead.

As a rough baseline: running four or five days a week, three or four of those should sit below LT1, typically 30–60 minutes, enough to build aerobic stimulus without real fatigue.

Quality Work: Raising the Ceiling

If the easy run builds the engine, quality work raises its ceiling. Quality sessions target LT2 and above, but different session types build different things.

Quality work needs a foundation first: hold off on structured quality sessions until you're running at least 15 miles a week and have logged 4–6 weeks of consistent, easy running; save harder VO2max-style intervals or higher-volume threshold work for 3+ months in. The 15-mile floor is arithmetic: coaching guidelines cap quality-paced volume at 8–10% of weekly mileage, so below that you're stuck between a session too small to count or one that blows past the cap. Before adding quality, build that foundation from four things:

  • Easy runs, to build your aerobic base without draining your legs.
  • Strides, short, controlled accelerations (typically 15–20 seconds, with full recovery between) that add speed and turnover without a heavy dose of stress.
  • Strength training, to build the capacity to handle more training stress as volume and intensity increase.
  • Consistency, to turn the effort from the other three into real, cumulative progress.

Intervals are short, hard repeats at RPE 8–9, sustainable for only a few minutes. They're the most direct, time-efficient way to raise VO2max, how much oxygen your muscles can use at full effort. Research on interval structure has shown that alternating short bursts near VO2max pace with brief recoveries lets runners accumulate far more time at VO2max per session than one continuous hard effort.4 Easy volume alone can build VO2max early on, but intervals are more efficient once a base exists.

One catch with intervals: heart rate lags behind effort by 30 seconds or more, so a short rep under two or three minutes can be well above threshold before your watch shows it. Trust pace and RPE for these: if you can't talk, you're working hard enough. Longer reps give heart rate time to catch up and confirm it. If a pace that used to spike your heart rate starts feeling manageable, that usually means fitness is improving, so adjust your targets. And if you're consistently falling short of the effort a session should produce, check whether easy days that crept into the gray zone are capping you before assuming the workout itself is the problem.

Tempo runs are sustained efforts at or near LT2, typically 20–40 minutes at RPE 7–8, training something different: how long you can hold hard effort before it falls apart. Most recreational runners undershoot tempo effort without realizing it, landing closer to LT1 than LT2. It should feel controlled early and earn its difficulty by the end. This is the deliberate moderate-intensity work a pyramidal week builds around.

Hill repeats are short, hard efforts (30 seconds to two minutes) up a moderate grade, with an easy jog back down as recovery. They build strength and power with less impact than flat intervals at the same effort.

For most runners, start with one quality session a week: a tempo run, sustained and controlled, without the complexity of intervals and recovery timing. Precede every quality session with at least ten minutes of easy running.

The Long Run: Time on Feet

The long run does a different job than either the easy run or quality work: sustained time on feet, building race-specific endurance through glycogen depletion and cumulative muscular fatigue that shorter runs don't produce. The payoff, better fat oxidation, glycogen sparing, muscular resilience, mental rehearsal, comes from running long at an easy, conversational effort; push too hard and the last few miles become a grind you spend days recovering from. Cardiac drift applies here too, so starting well below your target effort gives you headroom for heart rate to climb late in the run without crossing threshold.

How fast to build, and how much is too much: the biggest injury risk comes down to a sudden spike in a single run relative to your recent longest effort, more than weekly mileage or cumulative load. A large cohort study of over 5,000 runners found that increasing your longest run by 10–30% compared to your longest run in the prior month carried a 64% higher injury risk.5 Build gradually, no more than one to two miles per week.

How long the long run should actually be, by race distance:

Race Distance Long Run Guidance
5K Beginner plans typically peak at 3–4 miles; experienced runners chasing a specific time can push to 8–10, beyond which recovery cost outweighs the benefit for a race this short.
10K Recreational plans usually peak between 6–8 miles; experienced runners can push to around 12.
Half Marathon Recreational plans peak around 10–12 miles. A study of recreational runners found peak long runs beyond roughly 13 miles linked to faster finishes and less late-race pace decline; aim for 30–40% of weekly volume.6
Marathon The popular "3-hour cap" is a coaching convention rather than a research finding, and it penalizes slower runners by capping distance rather than actual stimulus. The same study, of over 400 recreational marathoners, found peak long runs below 15.5 miles linked to slower finishes, gains leveling off around 18.5–22 miles, and no added benefit, or added injury risk, beyond that.6 That injury risk comes specifically from sudden spikes; the study found long runs themselves carried no added risk at any distance.

For a practical time cap, adjust for your own pace: at a 10-minute pace, 18 miles takes about three hours; at 14 minutes, over four hours. A 3.5–4 hour working range gets most recreational runners into the distance the research supports without excessive recovery cost.

The long run belongs at the end of the training week, with an easy day or rest day before it and pure recovery after. It's a run the whole week supports, so that week has to actually be easy for the long run to do its job.

Crossing the Finish Line

If you take one thing away from all this, take this: run your easy runs truly easy. That's the single biggest lever most runners aren't pulling, and everything else here is secondary to getting it right. Beyond that, build the foundation, consistent volume, strides, strength work, weeks of easy running behind you, before you add quality. The gray zone is what happens when you skip either step: easy days that aren't easy, quality added before there's anything for it to build on. Get those two things right, and the rest of this — which model, which sessions, how to read your watch — is just detail.
