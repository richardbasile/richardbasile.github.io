Movement as Medicine

Your body and mind are built to heal, regulate, and thrive through motion. That you keep moving matters more than how you choose to move.

My choice is running and strength training. For others it might be walking, swimming, yoga, or riding a bike. The best movement is the one you'll keep doing.

When movement disappears from daily life, many systems gradually degrade. When it returns, in appropriate forms and doses, they recover. Movement isn't a lifestyle add-on. It's foundational.

Biological Regulation

When muscles contract, they send signals that ripple outward: inflammation drops, metabolism shifts, the immune system responds, joints circulate fluid, and bones respond to load. Whether those signals come from a run, a walk, a bike ride, or a strength session, what matters is that the signal is sent.

Regular movement tells your tissues to stay strong. Long gaps followed by sudden spikes lead to injury, because the body adapts precisely to what it is asked to do. You will learn this the hard way if you ignore it.

Nervous System Balance

Gentle, rhythmic motion calms the stress response: a walk that clears mental fog, laps in a pool that reset a bad mood, an easy row that empties out a cluttered head. A steady, predictable pace seems to matter more than the movement itself. The body reads the repetition as safety, and the nervous system follows.

More intense or skill-based movement sharpens attention and builds confidence. Holding a difficult yoga pose, tracking a fast rally in pickleball, or pushing through the last few minutes of a hard workout all demand a kind of focus that an easy walk doesn't. That focus carries over: the same concentration you bring to a hard pose or a tough rally is the attention you bring back to a difficult conversation or a stalled project at work.

Rest days matter, but too much rest works against you. Prolonged inactivity tends to worsen mood, increase stiffness, and make it harder to get moving again.

Meaning and Agency

Movement reinforces agency. When you choose to move, especially when motivation is low, each session becomes a quiet statement: I can act, and I can participate in my own care.

The right movement is the one that stays in your life when work is busy, motivation fades, or things get complicated. A back injury kept me from running for three years; physical therapy and cortisone injections didn't move the needle. What did help me return to running was something deceptively simple: daily long walks and gentle stretching. This experience taught me to keep an open mind and embrace the varied, everyday efforts that truly support long-term health.

The Long View

Movement is a lifetime practice, and the specific program changes as you do. Running gives way to hiking, cycling pairs with strength training, long workouts shrink into shorter daily routines. What stays constant is the need for regular reminders that life demands motion.
