Textual Criticism & Translation History

The Bible Translation Family Tree

Organized by underlying Greek New Testament textual basis, and by translation philosophy within each group

These three aren't equivalent categories of the same kind: Textus Receptus is a lineage of printed Greek editions starting with Erasmus; Majority/Byzantine Text is a reconstructed manuscript tradition; Critical Text is an editorial method applied across evolving scholarly editions (Nestle-Aland/UBS). They're grouped here as the three textual bases behind English Bible translations, not as three parallel manuscript families.

Key Differences

These textual bases disagree because they weigh different evidence: CT leans on a small number of old manuscripts and reasoned evaluation of each variant, while TR and MT lean on a much larger body of later ones. Part of the case for CT's approach is that later manuscripts accumulated centuries of scribal smoothing, harmonizing, and clarifying — a widely-held view among critical text scholars, though not one that MT or TR proponents accept as the deciding factor. Six of the best-known disputed passages, below.

None of these six create a theological conflict when read against the whole of Scripture. Even the most significant, the Comma Johanneum's explicit Trinity formula, doesn't establish or undermine the doctrine of the Trinity on its own either way: that doctrine rests on the cumulative witness of many passages (John 1:1, Matthew 28:19, 2 Corinthians 13:14, among others), not on any single verse. Textual variants can affect the wording, emphasis, or evidential support of individual passages without necessarily changing a doctrine considered from the whole biblical corpus — which is why major Christian doctrines generally shouldn't be evaluated from a single disputed reading in isolation. That principle is widely shared among textual critics across the theological spectrum, whichever family they personally favor.

The Comma Johanneum

1 John 5:7–8

Textus Receptus
Includes the explicit Trinity formula: "there are three that bear record in heaven, the Father, the Word, and the Holy Ghost: and these three are one."
Majority Text
Omits it: not found in the Byzantine manuscript majority.
Critical Text
Omits it: absent from every Greek manuscript before the 14th century.

The one major case where TR stands alone against both other families. Erasmus omitted it from his first two editions (1516, 1519) because it wasn't in his Greek manuscripts, then added it in 1522 after a Greek manuscript containing it (Codex 61) surfaced. The popular version of this story has Erasmus promising in advance to include the Comma if such a manuscript could be produced — a colorful anecdote repeated by many respected scholars, but one specialist research has found no solid basis for in Erasmus's own writings, so it's best treated as tradition rather than settled fact. What is well established: that manuscript is itself late, and its Greek is understood to derive from a Latin source rather than an independent Greek transmission. KJV and NKJV include the Comma; almost no other modern translation does.

The longer ending of Mark

Mark 16:9–20

Textus Receptus
Included as ordinary text: resurrection appearances, the Great Commission, snake-handling/poison verses.
Majority Text
Included: present in the vast majority of surviving manuscripts.
Critical Text
Absent from several of the earliest and most important Greek witnesses, including Codex Sinaiticus and Codex Vaticanus; other early evidence (patristic silence, stylistic differences from the rest of Mark) also raises questions about its originality. Usually printed but bracketed with a note.

Most CT-based translations (ESV, NIV, NASB) still print these verses, just flagged as likely a later addition rather than silently dropped.

The woman caught in adultery

John 7:53–8:11

Textus Receptus
Included as ordinary text.
Majority Text
Included: present in most later manuscripts, though its position varies across some.
Critical Text
Absent from the earliest manuscripts; usually printed but bracketed as a probable later insertion.

Same pattern as Mark 16: absent from the earliest Greek witnesses and appearing in different locations in some later manuscripts, which leads most modern critical editions to regard this well-loved, ancient story as a later addition to John's Gospel. Kept in most Bibles but flagged.

"God" vs. "He who"

1 Timothy 3:16

Textus Receptus
"God (Θεός) was manifest in the flesh," a direct statement of Christ's deity.
Majority Text
Same as TR: "God (Θεός) was manifest in the flesh."
Critical Text
"He who (Ὅς) was manifested in the flesh," a relative pronoun, not the noun "God."

A scribal abbreviation/letter-form issue, not simply one ordinary letter changed: ΘC (an abbreviation for θεός, "God") and OC (ὅς, "who") could look visually similar in the relevant manuscript hands, which is how the two readings are thought to have diverged. This passage has consequently featured prominently in debates over the textual basis of the KJV and the doctrine of Christ's deity, which doesn't rest on this verse alone in either case.

"Good will toward men"

Luke 2:14

Textus Receptus
εὐδοκία (nominative): "peace, good will toward men," implying goodwill toward everyone.
Majority Text
Same nominative form as TR.
Critical Text
εὐδοκίας (genitive): "peace to men on whom his favor rests," implying peace specifically to those God favors.

One letter (a final sigma) changes how the phrase is understood: the traditional nominative reading is usually rendered as goodwill toward everyone, while the genitive reading is typically understood as peace to those who receive God's favor. Which reading is original affects the grammar and translation; how each cashes out theologically is a further, separate question.

An added clause

Romans 8:1

Textus Receptus
Adds "who walk not after the flesh, but after the Spirit" at the end of the verse.
Majority Text
Also includes the added clause.
Critical Text
Omits the clause: verse ends at "no condemnation."

Many textual critics regard the longer reading as an expansion influenced by the similar wording later in verse 4, though this is an inference about how the variant arose rather than a directly attested historical event.

On the arrows

Each arrow between cards describes a specific kind of relationship, not just "came after":

  • Revised as — the child is the next edition of the same work: the same translation project continuing forward, with wording or text updated through an editorial revision cycle (e.g. Great Bible → Bishops' Bible, ASV → RSV).
  • Incorporated into — the parent's actual text was reused, largely unedited, as a component inside a different, separately-authored work, rather than revised on its own terms (e.g. Tyndale's completed books → Matthew's Bible).
  • Adapted as — the child is an independently produced new work, often a fresh translation from the original languages, that was deliberately modeled on the parent's wording or structure without being a formal revision of it or simply reusing its text (e.g. KJV → MEV).

On Formal / Mediating / Dynamic

Within each textual-basis family, translations are further grouped by translation philosophy — how closely the English tracks the original wording versus the original meaning:

  • Formal equivalence — translates as close to word-for-word as English allows, preserving the original sentence structure and syntax even where it reads less naturally (e.g. ESV, NASB).
  • Mediating (sometimes called "optimal equivalence") — formal by default, but shifting toward natural English phrasing wherever a strict word-for-word rendering would be confusing or misleading; not a uniform compromise applied line-by-line, but a deliberate attempt at both at once (e.g. NET Bible, HCSB, CSB).
  • Dynamic equivalence — translates thought-for-thought, prioritizing how a native English speaker would naturally express the same idea, even where that means restructuring the sentence (e.g. NLT, GNT).

On the ⚑ notes

Cards flagged with ⚑ have a documented theological, denominational, or doctrinal leaning behind specific translation choices: sponsoring institution, oversight-committee composition, or contested renderings (gender language, the divine name, etc.). This isn't a claim that every rendering in these translations is theologically motivated, or that unflagged translations have none; it's the set where a specific, sourceable leaning is well known and worth being aware of as a reader. Presented factually; where a translation choice was controversial, both the translators' stated rationale and their critics' objections are noted.
