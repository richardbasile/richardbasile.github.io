16-Week Half Marathon Build  ·  Aug 2 – Nov 15, 2026

Training Plan

Overview

Weekly mileage by day, building toward the Run to Feed the Hungry Half Marathon on Nov 14. This season's focus is distance and endurance; speed can be next season's project. Quality follows a 15-mile weekly floor (weeks under that run strides only) and targets half marathon race pace, growing from a quarter mile to a full mile so that pace feels familiar by race day. Tuesday is easy aerobic cross-training (swim or bike), 30–40 minutes depending on phase, dropping to 20 minutes in the taper and race weeks — except week 10, when Tuesday carries a run instead and cross-training is skipped that week.

Quality

Every quality rep targets half marathon race pace, not a faster or slower effort. Training for one specific race means the most valuable use of a limited number of hard sessions is rehearsing the exact effort race day demands, so it's a known feeling by Nov 14 rather than something guessed at cold. That's a departure from a polarized model (easy running plus genuinely hard VO2max work) — this plan spends its quality time in the moderate zone on purpose, because moderate effort *is* race effort here. It also matches this season's goal: distance and endurance, not speed. The volume progression builds that; quality reinforces it rather than chasing a faster ceiling. Speed can be next season's focus, once a bigger endurance base is in place.

The distance ladder — quarter mile, then half mile, up to a full mile at Peak — reflects that holding race pace for 30 seconds and for 8+ minutes are different skills at the same pace, so reps build at each distance before stepping up. Deload weeks hold the current distance rather than pushing further, consolidating rather than adding fatigue. The hard part of any session stays under a fifth of that week's total mileage — the same reason weeks under 15 miles skip quality for strides. Once a week clears that floor, quality kicks in and stays for the rest of the build. Taper reverts to the shortest version, keeping the pace familiar without adding late fatigue. Race weeks skip the structure entirely — the race itself is the ultimate rehearsal.

Strength Program

Three sessions per week — Monday, Thursday, and Friday — with a fixed exercise list that stays the same for the whole plan — only sets, reps, and load change by phase. Monday is full-body and paired with the day's easy run: barbell squat and deadlift alternate which one is loaded heavy each week, plus bench press, banded rows, and skip rope for plyometric calf work — this is the day with the most recovery runway before Wednesday's quality run and Saturday's long run. Thursday is lower body, also paired with that day's easy run, kept deliberately light and controlled: goblet squat, walking lunge, single-leg RDL, Bulgarian split squat, and step-ups, plus a dead bug/bird dog core finisher. Friday is upper body: banded rows, shoulder press, banded lateral raises, DB flyes, push-ups, and curls, plus a dedicated core/hip finisher (side plank, dead bug, lateral leg raise, clamshell) — this is where anti-rotation and anti-extension core work is concentrated for the week, since Monday's core exposure comes from bracing under load rather than dedicated core work. Fixed exercise list, explicit each week: Monday, Thursday, and Friday keep the same exercise categories for the entire 16 weeks — Monday is squat, deadlift, bench press, banded rows, and skip rope; Thursday is goblet squat, walking lunge, single-leg RDL, Bulgarian split squat, step-ups, and the dead bug/bird dog core finisher; Friday is banded rows, shoulder press, banded lateral raise, DB flyes, push-ups, DB curls, and the side plank/dead bug/lateral leg raise/clamshell core finisher. Which lower-body lift is loaded heavy on Monday is called out explicitly every single week rather than left to "alternate" — squat-emphasis and deadlift-emphasis weeks trade off, so both lifts get even progression and there's nothing to remember. Only sets, reps, and load change week to week — the exercises themselves never get swapped out.

Weeks 1–9 (Foundation/Build): Working sets and progressive load, but only one of squat or deadlift progresses on any given Monday — the other holds at a light, technical load. Squat-emphasis and deadlift-emphasis weeks alternate, so you never add load to both lower-body lifts in the same session. Thursday's lower-body work stays light and controlled throughout — including the Bulgarian split squat, kept to bodyweight or light dumbbells rather than progressing load — and Friday stays zero leg loading, because Friday sits the day before Saturday's long run and lower-body soreness peaks 24–48 hours after a session — exactly the window that would otherwise land on the long run.

Week 10 exception: Tuesday carries a 3-mile run that week instead of Monday, so Monday's strength session is dropped entirely rather than stacked with a shifted schedule — Thursday and Friday both run as usual, with no substitute added for the missed Monday session.

Weeks 11–14 (Peak): As running volume climbs to 22–26 mi/week, strength shifts to maintenance across all three days — same exercise list and working weight, fewer sets, squat and deadlift both held steady rather than progressing, and Thursday trims volume across the board. The goal is holding onto what's been built, not progressing further, while running takes priority.

Week 15 (Taper) and Week 16 (Race): Taper week runs one light, single-set pass through the full list on all three days — Monday, Thursday, and Friday — just to feel normal, nothing more. The race is still 8–9 days out from this week's sessions, so there's room for a light touch without borrowing from race freshness. Race week (16) is where the real freshness buffer lives — it skips strength entirely, since the race is that week's only output.
